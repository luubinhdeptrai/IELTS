# English Foundation — 3-Month Roadmap

**Phase 1: build practical English for everyday life.**  
**Time:** 120 minutes daily, seven days weekly.  
**Duration:** 12 topic weeks + six consolidation days = 90 days.  
**Starting point:** rebuild weak foundations while using the English you already know.

## 1. Purpose of This Phase

For these three months, focus on understanding people, expressing ideas, enjoying readable English, and writing clearly about your life. Success means that familiar English becomes easier to understand and use without preparing every sentence.

Your previous overall result of approximately 5.5 suggests that you already have useful knowledge. It does not tell us which everyday skills are automatic. You may understand a grammar explanation but struggle to use the pattern in conversation. You may know a word on paper but fail to recognize it in speech. This phase connects that knowledge to real use.

The objective is **English ability rather than IELTS exam ability**. Exam practice develops familiarity with an examination; this phase develops the language that you will eventually bring to preparation. Your daily questions are:

- Can I understand the main idea?
- Can I explain something from my own life?
- Can I respond to an unexpected follow-up question?
- Can I use yesterday's language again today?

Borrow children's helpful learning habits: repeated exposure, meaningful situations, imitation, curiosity, and willingness to make mistakes. Add adult advantages: brief explanations, deliberate review, focused pronunciation work, and feedback. Choose content that interests an adult; learning naturally does not require using children's materials.

## 2. Core Learning Philosophy

### The learning loop

```text
Input → Understanding → Imitation → Production → Feedback → Repetition
```

| Step | What you do | Everyday example |
|---|---|---|
| Input | Hear or read useful English. | Listen to someone ordering lunch. |
| Understanding | Connect the language to the situation. | Understand what the customer wants. |
| Imitation | Copy a short, understood model. | Repeat “Could I have some water, please?” |
| Production | Change the model to express your idea. | Ask for tea instead of water. |
| Feedback | Check whether your message and wording work. | Notice that “some water” fits better than “a water” here. |
| Repetition | Retrieve and reuse it on later days. | Order a different drink in another role-play. |

Meaning comes first. Analyze one useful feature after understanding the message, then use it. A perfect explanation of a rule is less useful than being able to use that rule when needed.

### Build a direct connection to meaning

You may currently follow this route:

```text
English → Vietnamese → understanding
apple → quả táo → the fruit
```

Repeated, meaningful exposure can make a shorter route more familiar:

```text
English → meaning / image / idea
apple → 🍎
“take a break” → the experience of stopping work and resting
```

An image works for concrete words. A situation, feeling, action, or simple English explanation works for abstract language. When you hear “I'm running late,” imagine someone hurrying because an appointment is about to start.

Vietnamese remains a useful tool. A quick translation can clarify a difficult meaning. After that, return to the English sentence and its situation. The aim is to reduce compulsory sentence-by-sentence translation, not to prohibit your first language.

### Ten working principles

1. **Comprehensible input:** choose material you mostly understand, with a small challenge.
2. **Useful phrases:** learn “make a decision,” not just “decision.”
3. **Repeated encounters:** bring important language back through listening, reading, speaking, and writing.
4. **Active production:** speak and write every day, including review days.
5. **Gradual difficulty:** change one challenge at a time: length, speed, vocabulary, or topic.
6. **Meaning before rules:** understand the message before inspecting its grammar.
7. **Context:** connect language to a topic, situation, and personal example.
8. **Consistency:** keep the two-hour limit; avoid accumulating catch-up sessions.
9. **Automaticity:** retrieve familiar language without looking, then reuse it in a new situation.
10. **Foundation first:** measure communication and understanding throughout this phase.

Use review after a delay, recall before checking, and feedback followed by another attempt. The intervals and activity lengths below are practical defaults, not a universal formula.

## 3. What Not to Focus on Yet

Leave examination materials, score targets, memorized exam answers, essay templates, and exam strategies outside this phase. Use daily conversations, messages, stories, explanations, and short articles instead.

Also avoid collecting resources without using them, memorizing huge isolated word lists, analyzing every sentence, and watching difficult videos for two hours without producing English.

1. Complete the first-day baseline below.
2. Find your current topic in the 12-week table.
3. Follow the Monday–Sunday schedule using that topic.

## 4. Overview of the Three Months

The monthly stages use four-week learning blocks: Days 1–28, 29–56, and 57–84. Days 85–90 consolidate Month 3. This avoids treating 12 weeks as if they were exactly 90 days.

### Month 1 — Build the Base

**Objective:** connect familiar words and basic patterns to sound, meaning, and everyday use.

| Area | Focus and expected ability |
|---|---|
| Listening | Short, clear A1–A2/A2 dialogues, usually 1–3 minutes. Identify who, where, what, and a few details. |
| Speaking | Move from single sentences to 2–3 connected sentences, short exchanges, and a 45–60-second description. |
| Reading | Easy messages, descriptions, and graded stories. Read 150–300-word texts or a short section of an easy reader. |
| Writing | Build complete sentences, then a coherent 60–100-word paragraph. |
| Vocabulary | Personal information, family, home, routines, study, hobbies; usually 3–5 useful items per learning day. |
| Grammar | Repair `be`, pronouns, articles, plurals, present forms, questions, negatives, and basic prepositions. |
| Pronunciation | Check meaningful sound contrasts, final consonants, plural endings, basic word stress, and contractions. |
| Recommended activities | Listen–check–relisten, short dictation, pause-and-repeat, picture descriptions, personal journals, easy reading. |
| Difficulty | Mostly comfortable material. Use A1 briefly for a specific gap; do not force yourself through material you already control. |
| Progress indicators | Less script dependence; clearer endings; longer understandable sentence groups; recognition of familiar phrases in audio. |

**Weekly evidence:** one saved recording, one corrected paragraph, one listening summary, and a manageable phrase set.

### Month 2 — Expand and Connect

**Objective:** connect ideas across time, give reasons, and participate in short conversations.

| Area | Focus and expected ability |
|---|---|
| Listening | A2 and accessible B1 dialogues, learner podcasts, and short stories, usually 3–5 minutes. Follow sequence, reasons, and speaker intentions. |
| Speaking | Retell an event, make arrangements, ask follow-up questions, and explain something for 1–2 minutes. |
| Reading | Easy stories and 250–450-word articles about familiar interests. Continue an enjoyable reader. |
| Writing | Write 100–160 words: a journal, practical message, description, or short story with clear connections. |
| Vocabulary | Food, shopping, transport, health, travel; retrieve Month 1 language while adding common collocations and phrasal verbs. |
| Grammar | Past simple, everyday future forms, comparisons, modals, quantity, present perfect, and connectors. |
| Pronunciation | Past `-ed`, consonant clusters, sentence stress, schwa/weak forms, and consonant-to-vowel linking. |
| Recommended activities | Retelling, information-gap role-plays, repeated speaking, sentence combining, podcast segments, delayed vocabulary recall. |
| Difficulty | Keep familiar material as the majority; introduce slightly longer or less scripted input one feature at a time. |
| Progress indicators | Clearer past/future meaning; more relevant follow-ups; fewer long pauses for familiar phrases; better recognition of reduced words. |

**Connection to Month 1:** use the same family, routine, and hobby language to discuss yesterday, next weekend, comparisons, and problems. Add layers to known language rather than replacing it.

### Month 3 — Use English More Naturally

**Objective:** transfer practiced English to new but familiar situations with less preparation.

| Area | Focus and expected ability |
|---|---|
| Listening | Accessible B1 learner material, usually 4–8 minutes, plus selected 30–90-second natural clips with a clear speaker and familiar topic. |
| Speaking | Give 2–3-minute explanations and sustain roughly 8–10 minutes of supported everyday conversation, including clarification. |
| Reading | Read 400–650-word accessible general-interest texts; try easier authentic lifestyle, technology, science, or culture articles selectively. |
| Writing | Produce 160–220 words in one or two connected paragraphs; revise for meaning and recurring errors. |
| Vocabulary | Technology, entertainment, work, relationships, plans; strengthen earlier phrases and flexible paraphrasing. |
| Grammar | Useful relative clauses, zero/first conditionals, common verb patterns, basic passive recognition, and familiar complex sentences. |
| Pronunciation | Combine clear sounds, endings, stress, rhythm, intonation, and connected-speech recognition in real speech. |
| Recommended activities | New-situation role-plays, no-script summaries, short opinions, problem-solving conversations, integrated personal projects. |
| Difficulty | Mostly understandable learner input. Short natural clips supplement it when meaning remains accessible. |
| Progress indicators | You recover from missing a word; express reasons and examples; reuse language across topics; translate less in familiar situations. |

**Connection to Month 2:** retain past/future narration and reasons, then add description, conditions, explanations, and spontaneous responses. Natural use grows through transfer, not through an abrupt switch to difficult native content.

**Adjustment rule:** if a stage is too difficult, keep its topic and activity but shorten the input and output. If it is easy, remove notes, change the situation, or add a follow-up question before increasing language difficulty.

## 5. Listening Roadmap

### Comprehensible input

For focused listening, start with material where you understand approximately **70–90% of the overall message** before studying it. This is a rough selection guide, not a precise word-count threshold. For relaxed listening, choose easier material so the story remains enjoyable.

```text
Short beginner explanations, if needed
    ↓
Clear A1–A2 / A2 conversations
    ↓
Slow learner English and short stories
    ↓
Accessible learner podcasts and simple explanatory videos
    ↓
Brief, clear natural conversations on familiar topics
```

These are options, not a required sequence of separate courses. A learner podcast may be easier than a cartoon; a cartoon or sitcom can contain fast speech, jokes, and cultural references.

**Choose a recording in three minutes:**

1. Pick a topic you know and a source with a transcript or reliable English captions.
2. Listen to 45–60 seconds without text.
3. Explain who is speaking, what is happening, and two details.
4. If you understand the situation but miss some details, it is useful focused material.
5. If you cannot identify the situation, choose a shorter/easier recording or preview two essential words.
6. If everything is effortless across several recordings, increase one feature: length, speed, vocabulary, or number of speakers.

After about three successful sessions at the current difficulty, try one slightly harder sample. Keep it only if understanding remains workable. Topic familiarity matters as much as a level label.

### Intensive listening: a 20–25-minute procedure

1. **First listen:** play a 2–5-minute recording without text. Write the main idea and three details.
2. **Second listen:** answer your own questions about the situation; do not pause every sentence.
3. **Check:** read the transcript or use English captions. Locate the sections you misunderstood.
4. **Investigate:** choose two difficult lines. Was the problem an unknown phrase, a weak sound, linking, or losing your place?
5. **Replay:** hear those lines again, then the full recording without text.
6. **Transfer:** give a brief spoken summary in your speaking block. Save no more than 3–5 useful phrases in your vocabulary block.

### Dictation

**Default:** 5–8 minutes on Monday–Saturday, inside the listening block. Sunday uses a brief familiar excerpt for review if useful. Dictation should occupy a small part of listening, not replace understanding longer messages.

1. Select **5–15 seconds** of clear, familiar audio with a transcript. Start with 5 seconds if necessary.
2. Listen once without writing; understand the situation.
3. Listen again and write exactly what you hear. Leave blanks instead of inventing words.
4. Replay two or three times. Split the excerpt into smaller meaningful pieces if needed.
5. Compare with the transcript after approximately three attempts; avoid spending the whole block on one line.
6. Mark omitted words, incorrect sounds, endings, and spelling separately.
7. Decide why each important mismatch happened.
8. Listen while reading the correct line; then listen without text.
9. Say the line once and explain its meaning.

| What happened? | Likely issue | Next action |
|---|---|---|
| You know the written word but missed it. | Sound recognition, linking, or weak form. | Compare the sound with the written phrase and replay in context. |
| You did not know the expression. | Vocabulary/meaning gap. | Learn the whole useful expression and a personal example. |
| You missed `a`, `to`, or `have`. | Unstressed grammatical word. | Listen for the phrase rhythm, then its quieter words. |
| You missed plural `-s` or past `-ed`. | Ending recognition or grammar expectation. | Check sound and meaning, then repeat the sentence. |
| You heard the word but wrote it incorrectly. | Spelling gap. | Check spelling; do not label this a listening failure. |

Dictation can help connect speech to familiar vocabulary, connected speech, weak sounds, spelling, and grammatical endings. Always finish by understanding the sentence; transcription accuracy alone is not comprehension.

### Repeating and shadowing

| Method | What you do | Best starting use |
|---|---|---|
| Repeating / echoing | Listen, pause, then say the sentence. | Learn the sounds and rhythm of a short model. |
| Shadowing | Speak along with the recording, usually a fraction behind it. | Practice keeping up with familiar rhythm and phrasing. |

**Default:** 5–8 minutes within the speaking block on Monday–Saturday. Start with repeating; simultaneous shadowing is optional until the text is easy to understand.

1. Choose a **10–30-second passage** whose meaning you understand.
2. Listen twice. Mark stressed words and phrase boundaries.
3. Pause after each phrase and imitate its sounds, stress, rhythm, and intonation.
4. Record one version; compare one feature, such as final consonants.
5. Repeat the passage in larger groups.
6. When comfortable, speak along with the audio, slightly behind it. Stop if keeping up destroys clarity or understanding.
7. Turn off the audio and express the same idea about yourself.

**Month 1:** mostly pause-and-repeat. **Month 2:** shadow familiar 10–20-second passages. **Month 3:** try 20–40 seconds of comfortable speech. Return to repeating whenever needed.

Shadowing copies a model; conversation requires choosing your own message. Always leave most of the speaking block for your own English.

### Extensive listening

Use approximately **10–15 minutes on Tuesday, Thursday, and Saturday**, inside their listening blocks. Listen for enjoyment without dictating or collecting every unfamiliar word.

Suitable options include a learner podcast segment, an easy short story, a simple educational video, a familiar daily conversation, or a clear cooking demonstration. In Month 3, try an accessible documentary or sitcom clip briefly if you can follow it. Rewatching something enjoyable is useful.

Afterward, say one sentence about what happened. If you repeatedly lose the story, choose easier content. Background English can be an optional micro-habit, but focused, understood listening remains the core.

### Subtitle strategy

| Support | Practical rule |
|---|---|
| No subtitles | Use for the first short attempt and the final replay. Also use throughout easy extensive listening when meaning stays clear. |
| English subtitles / transcript | Use to check specific misunderstandings and connect sound to wording. Listen again after removing them. |
| Vietnamese subtitles | Use briefly when an essential meaning remains unclear. Then return to the English line and replay without Vietnamese. Avoid reading Vietnamese throughout the study recording. |

Prefer human-edited transcripts. Automatic captions can mishear words; do not treat every caption as a correct model. If needed, use about 0.85–0.9× playback briefly, then finish with the original speed. Choose easier input if you constantly need substantial slowing.

## 6. Speaking Roadmap

### Daily-life progression

| Weeks | What to produce | Concrete practice |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Simple sentences → 2–3 connected sentences. | Describe yourself, your family, a room, or a normal morning. |
| 3–4 | Short exchanges → 45–60-second descriptions. | Discuss classes, studying, hobbies, homework, and preferences. |
| 5–6 | 6–10-turn practical conversations. | Order food, ask prices, compare clothes, and ask directions. |
| 7–8 | 1–2-minute retellings and explanations. | Describe a health habit, tell a trip story, and arrange a weekend. |
| 9–10 | 2-minute explanations and simple opinions. | Explain an app, recommend a movie, or discuss a work/study problem. |
| 11–12 | 2–3-minute explanations and 8–10-minute supported conversations. | Discuss plans, solve a practical problem, and respond to unfamiliar follow-ups. |

These are task lengths, not requirements to speak without any pause. A useful clarification question counts as successful communication.

### A speaking block: approximately 20–30 minutes

1. Spend 5–8 minutes repeating/shadowing a short understood model.
2. Choose a real prompt from the weekly topic. Prepare **three keywords**, not a full script.
3. Speak for the week's target duration and record it.
4. Replay a short part; choose one wording issue and one pronunciation issue.
5. Speak again with the same meaning but some different details.
6. Answer two follow-up questions without notes.

If you cannot recall a word, describe it: “the thing I use to charge my phone.” Practice recovering rather than restarting every sentence.

### Common phrases: learn chunks

Instead of only **depend**, learn:

```text
It depends on the weather.
That depends.
It depends on the situation.
```

Instead of only **opinion**, start with **“I think…”** and **“In my opinion…”**. “From my perspective…” is useful later; “As far as I'm concerned…” can sound firmer and is not needed in every conversation. Choose wording that suits the situation.

| Function | Useful examples |
|---|---|
| Agreeing | “I agree.” / “That makes sense.” |
| Disagreeing politely | “I'm not sure I agree.” / “I see your point, but…” |
| Asking for clarification | “Could you say that again?” / “Do you mean…?” |
| Giving opinions | “I think…” / “In my opinion…” |
| Expressing uncertainty | “I'm not sure.” / “It might be…” |
| Describing feelings | “I'm a bit worried about…” / “I feel relieved.” |
| Preferences | “I'd rather…” / “I prefer tea to coffee.” |
| Making requests | “Could you help me with…?” / “Could I have…?” |
| Making suggestions | “How about…?” / “Why don't we…?” |
| Apologizing | “Sorry I'm late.” / “Sorry, I misunderstood.” |
| Thanking | “Thanks for your help.” / “I really appreciate it.” |
| Starting conversations | “How's your day going?” / “What have you been up to?” |
| Ending conversations | “I'd better get going.” / “It was nice talking to you.” |
| Reacting naturally | “Really?” / “That sounds great.” / “That's a shame.” |
| Buying something | “How much is this?” / “Do you have this in a larger size?” |
| Ordering food | “I'd like the chicken, please.” / “Could we have the bill, please?” |
| Asking directions | “How do I get to…?” / “Is it within walking distance?” |
| Discussing plans | “Are you free on Saturday?” / “Let's meet at…” |

Choose **2–3 functions per week** that fit your topic. Learn a few expressions well rather than memorizing this whole table at once. Retrieve each expression, change one detail, and use it in an exchange.

### Self-talk

Use real objects and actions during speaking time:

```text
This is my desk. I usually study here.
I'm checking tomorrow's timetable.
I need to finish my homework before dinner.
This app is useful because it helps me organize my tasks.
```

Build a message, not a stream of disconnected labels: name something, describe it, give a reason, then add one detail.

### Conversation practice

Use **Wednesday and Saturday speaking blocks** for interaction. A friend, classmate, exchange partner, or tutor is helpful; a native speaker is not required. Use English for the activity even if both partners speak Vietnamese.

**Conversation procedure:**

1. Choose a purpose: order lunch, arrange a trip, choose a movie, or explain a problem.
2. Take different roles and keep the conversation moving.
3. Ask at least three relevant follow-up questions.
4. Include one information gap: a budget, preference, time, or problem the other person does not know.
5. Discuss feedback afterward; repeat a short part with improvements.

**If you are alone:** prepare six short question cards, shuffle them, and answer without a script. Play both roles in a practical dialogue, changing a constraint on the second attempt. This practices response-building, although real interaction remains valuable when available.

Do not count a copied monologue as all your speaking practice. Include personally chosen sentences and unexpected questions every week.

## 7. Pronunciation Roadmap

The goal is understandable, comfortable speech and better listening. You do not need to erase your Vietnamese accent. Select a consistent clear model for imitation while listening to several speakers over time.

### Priorities

| Stage | What to practice | Examples and checks |
|---|---|---|
| Month 1 | Sound contrasts that cause confusion; final consonants; plural/third-person endings; word stress. | Compare `ship/sheep`, `thin/tin`, or `rice/rise` only if you confuse them. Keep the ending in `like`, `likes`, and `liked`. |
| Month 2 | Past `-ed`; consonant clusters; sentence stress; contractions; weak forms; linking. | Practice `worked`, `played`, `wanted`; connect `turn‿it‿off`; hear quieter words in `a cup of tea`. |
| Month 3 | Intonation, rhythm, phrasing, connected-speech recognition, and transfer into spontaneous speech. | Explain a problem, highlight the important information, and check that endings remain understandable. |

For Vietnamese speakers, final sounds/clusters, contrasts such as /θ/–/t/ or /ð/–/d/, /s/–/ʃ/, and some vowel contrasts can be useful checks. Your accent and first-language variety affect which ones matter. Use recordings and feedback to identify **your** priorities.

### Endings: follow sounds, not letters

| Ending | Pronunciation | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Plural / third-person `-s` | /s/ after voiceless non-sibilant sounds. | `books`, `likes` |
| Plural / third-person `-s` | /z/ after vowels and voiced non-sibilant sounds. | `days`, `plays` |
| Plural / third-person `-s` | /ɪz/ or /əz/ after sibilant sounds such as /s z ʃ ʒ tʃ dʒ/. | `buses`, `watches` |
| Regular past `-ed` | /t/ after voiceless sounds except /t/. | `worked`, `washed` |
| Regular past `-ed` | /d/ after vowels and voiced sounds except /d/. | `played`, `cleaned` |
| Regular past `-ed` | /ɪd/ or /əd/ after /t/ or /d/. | `wanted`, `needed` |

Usually only the last category in each group adds a syllable. Do not add an extra vowel to every final consonant. In natural speech, some final stops have little audible release; clear speech does not require exaggerating every sound.

### Stress, rhythm, and intonation

- **Word stress:** one syllable is more prominent: `TAble`, `beGIN`, `imPORtant`. Check new words with dictionary audio.
- **Sentence stress:** emphasize the information you want the listener to notice: “I wanted the **BLUE** shirt.”
- **Rhythm:** English commonly alternates more prominent words with quieter, shorter grammatical words. Avoid forcing every syllable to the same length.
- **Intonation:** pitch helps show a statement, a genuine question, interest, or uncertainty. Copy meaningful examples rather than applying one rule to all questions.
- **Contractions:** practice ordinary forms such as `I'm`, `don't`, `can't`, `I've`, and `I'll` in complete sentences.
- **Weak forms:** words such as `to`, `and`, and `of` may become less prominent; the schwa /ə/ is a common unstressed vowel.

### Understand connected speech without forcing slang

| What you may hear | What it represents | Appropriate use |
|---|---|---|
| `want to` sounding like `wanna` | A common casual reduction before a verb. | Recognize it in listening. Use clearly pronounced “want to” when clarity or formality calls for it. |
| `going to` sounding like `gonna` | A reduction of the future construction before a verb. | “I'm going to study” may be reduced. “I'm going to university” describes movement/destination and does not become “I'm gonna university.” |
| `did you` sounding like `didja` | Sounds may merge in quick speech. | Recognize the spoken phrase; write “did you.” Clear “did you” is always useful. |

These spellings illustrate sounds; they are not required vocabulary for formal writing. Connected speech also occurs in careful speech, but its degree varies. Practice natural, clear phrasing without manufacturing a casual accent. For formal writing, retain standard forms; in presentations, choose the clarity and register the situation needs.

### An 8–10-minute pronunciation block

1. Listen to one target sound or sentence for 1 minute.
2. Compare two words or phrases for 2 minutes; check mouth position and voicing if relevant.
3. Practice three meaningful sentences for 3 minutes.
4. Record and compare for 2 minutes.
5. If time remains, use the target in a new sentence about the weekly topic.

Work on one main feature at a time. Use [BBC's pronunciation lessons](https://feeds.bbci.co.uk/learningenglish/english/features/pronunciation/) for sounds and connected-speech demonstrations, and [Cambridge audio](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/us/pronunciation/) for individual words.

## 8. Reading Roadmap

### Choose a gradual path

```text
Easy graded texts and everyday messages
    ↓
Graded short stories / easy readers
    ↓
Simple lifestyle, education, and culture articles
    ↓
Accessible general-interest articles
    ↓
Selected short news, technology, or science articles
```

Start at the easiest level that still interests you. Authentic news, children's articles, and simple Wikipedia pages vary considerably in difficulty; none is automatically easy. Choose by your actual understanding.

For relaxed reading, aim to recognize **almost all the words**—roughly 95–98%, preferably near the upper end. This differs from the rough listening-message guide. If each 100 words contains more than about five unfamiliar words, the text probably belongs in intensive reading or should be replaced with an easier version.

### Intensive reading

Use **Monday, Wednesday, and Friday reading blocks**, normally 15–20 minutes each.

1. Read a short text once for meaning without opening a dictionary.
2. Write its main idea in one sentence.
3. Reread one useful paragraph. Mark at most three phrases and one sentence pattern.
4. Look up language that blocks meaning, repeats, or is useful to you.
5. Identify the main subject and verb in one confusing sentence; divide it into meaningful parts.
6. Close the text and explain the paragraph simply.
7. Reuse one phrase or pattern in your writing block.

Do not translate the entire passage into Vietnamese. A targeted translation of a difficult sentence is acceptable; then reread it in English while imagining its meaning.

### Extensive reading

Use **Tuesday, Thursday, and Saturday reading blocks**, normally 15–20 minutes each.

1. Choose a story or article you can follow comfortably.
2. Read for the whole block with few interruptions.
3. Mark an interesting unknown word lightly and continue unless it blocks understanding.
4. Finish with one sentence about what happened or what interested you.

Continue the same book across sessions. Repeated characters and situations make language more familiar. A realistic aim is **one short reader or equivalent collection of stories per month**, adjusted to its length and your pace. Completing an enjoyable easier book is more useful than abandoning several difficult ones.

Free options include [British Council A2–B1 stories](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/general/story-zone/a2-b1). For a longer graded book, [Oxford Bookworms](https://www.oup.com.au/elt/oxford-bookworms) offers levels for secondary and adult learners; borrow a copy or buy one suitable title if affordable.

### Dictionary strategy

```text
Does the word prevent understanding? → Look it up.
Does it keep appearing?              → Consider looking it up.
Will I probably use it?              → Look it up and save the phrase.
Otherwise                           → Keep reading.
```

During intensive reading, cap lookups at approximately **3–5 per text**. During extensive reading, aim for **0–2 per session**, unless an essential word blocks the story. These are attention-management limits, not strict laws. Frequent necessary lookups mean you should choose an easier text.

Read a simple English definition first. Use Vietnamese briefly if it is still unclear. Check the meaning that fits the passage, listen to the pronunciation, and save only useful language.

## 9. Writing Roadmap

### Progression

```text
Complete simple sentences
    ↓
Sentences joined with and / but / because
    ↓
Short paragraphs and journals
    ↓
Descriptions, practical messages, and stories
    ↓
Opinions and explanations in longer connected paragraphs
```

| Stage | Typical output | Main goal |
|---|---|---|
| Month 1 | 5–8 sentences, then approximately 60–100 words. | Clear subjects, verbs, punctuation, and one main topic. |
| Month 2 | Approximately 100–160 words. | Sequence, reasons, examples, and understandable time references. |
| Month 3 | Approximately 160–220 words in one or two paragraphs. | Connected ideas, relevant detail, and revision of recurring errors. |

Word ranges are guides. A clear shorter message is better than padding. On a short writing day, produce a smaller message; complete longer pieces in the 20-minute blocks.

### Sentence patterns across the months

| Month | Patterns | Personal examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | `I am ___.` `There is/are ___.` `I usually ___ at ___.` `I like ___ because ___.` `I usually ___ when ___.` | “I usually study after dinner.” “I like cooking because it helps me relax.” |
| 2 | `Yesterday, I ___.` `I'm going to ___.` `___ is ___ than ___.` `One reason is that ___.` `If I have time, I ___.` `I've been interested in ___ since ___.` | “One reason is that the bus is cheaper.” “I've been interested in programming since high school.” |
| 3 | `I used to ___, but now ___.` `Although ___, I still ___.` `___ is something that ___.` `If ___, I will ___.` `The reason why ___ is that ___.` | “Although I'm busy, I still take a short walk.” “The reason why I use this app is that it saves time.” |

Introduce a pattern only when its meaning is useful. “The reason why…” is available, but simple “because” is often enough. Do not put a complex pattern in every sentence.

For “If I have time, I study,” describe a usual habit. For a specific future possibility, use “If I have time tomorrow, I'll study.”

### Daily journal

In Month 1, describe a typical day in the present, or use a few supported past forms for a completed day:

```text
I usually wake up at 7 a.m.
I have breakfast with my family.
After that, I go to university.
I study in the library after class.
My favorite place to study is quiet and comfortable.
```

In Month 2, narrate a completed day:

```text
Today I woke up at 7 a.m. and had breakfast with my family.
After that, I went to university. I had three classes.
The most interesting class was programming because we built a small app.
I felt tired afterward, so I took a short break.
```

In Month 3, add reflection:

```text
Although the assignment was difficult, I enjoyed solving the problem.
I used to give up quickly, but now I try another approach first.
If I get stuck tomorrow, I'll ask a classmate to explain their method.
```

Write a journal on **Tuesday, Thursday, and Saturday**. On other days, use the weekly writing task or a practical message. Every Sunday, revise one earlier piece.

### A 20-minute writing block

1. **3 minutes:** choose a reader/purpose and note three ideas in English.
2. **10 minutes:** draft without translating a Vietnamese paragraph or checking every sentence.
3. **4 minutes:** reread for meaning, sentence boundaries, and the week's grammar focus.
4. **3 minutes:** improve two sentences and record one recurring error if relevant.

For a 15-minute block, use 2 + 7 + 4 + 2 minutes. Keep your original draft so that revision remains visible.

### Paragraph building

Use a flexible structure:

```text
Main idea → useful detail → example → optional closing thought
```

Example: describe where you study, explain why it works, give an example of a recent session, then mention an improvement you want to make. This is a communication scaffold, not a memorized essay template.

Once or twice weekly, get feedback from a teacher, capable partner, or [Write & Improve](https://writeandimprove.com/free). Ask for the two or three most useful corrections and rewrite those sentences yourself. Automatic feedback is a starting point; check confusing suggestions with a reliable reference or teacher.

## 10. Grammar Roadmap

Keep explicit grammar to **8–10 minutes in the scheduled block**. Then use the pattern in that day's speaking and writing. Revisit familiar grammar quickly; spend more attention on recurring gaps.

### High-frequency progression

| Month | Priority topics | Communication purpose |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | `be`; subject/object/possessive forms; articles; singular/plural; `there is/are`; present simple; present continuous; basic questions/negatives; place/time prepositions. | Identify people and things, describe routines and current actions, ask everyday questions. |
| 2 | Past simple; useful past continuous; future `going to`, arrangements, and `will`; comparatives/superlatives; `can`, `could`, `should`, `have to`; countability/quantity; present perfect; `and`, `but`, `because`, `so`, `when`. | Tell stories, compare choices, request help, give advice, and discuss experiences/plans. |
| 3 | Defining relative clauses; zero/first conditionals; common gerund/infinitive patterns; `used to`; `although`; basic passive; useful `that`, `if`, and `whether` clauses. | Explain, identify, discuss consequences, contrast habits, and understand everyday explanations. |

Do not aim to master every exception. Introduce basic passive mainly for recognition and a few useful sentences, such as “The app is updated every month.” Keep more time for common active sentences.

### The grammar-use cycle

```text
Learn the pattern
    ↓
Understand three examples
    ↓
Notice it in today's listening/reading
    ↓
Create three personal sentences
    ↓
Use it in conversation/writing
    ↓
Recall it on a later day
```

**Example: past simple**

1. Notice “She cooked dinner yesterday.”
2. Understand that the event is finished and located in the past.
3. Compare “I cook every day” with “I cooked yesterday.”
4. Create three sentences about yesterday.
5. Tell your partner what happened after class.
6. Check one repeated mistake; tell a different past event tomorrow.

Within a grammar block, read a short explanation, complete **3–5 diagnostic items if needed**, and make personal examples. Correct exercise answers do not demonstrate spontaneous use; the speaking/writing blocks provide that practice.

For present perfect, connect experience or a continuing situation to now: “I've visited Da Nang twice” or “I've lived here since 2023.” Use past simple for finished-time statements such as “I visited Da Nang last summer.”

## 11. Vocabulary Roadmap

### Learn useful items in context

Actively learn **3–5 new useful words or phrases on Monday–Saturday**: roughly 18–30 weekly. An item may be a word with its common phrase, not necessarily a new isolated word. Sunday introduces no new items; Week 12 and Days 85–90 emphasize consolidation.

If reviews become difficult to finish, reduce to 2–3 items or pause new cards. You will encounter more vocabulary than you deliberately learn. You do not need to save all of it.

For each selected item, collect:

```text
word/phrase + pronunciation + meaning in this context
    + common collocation/chunk + source example + personal sentence
```

Example:

```text
Item: exhausted
Part of speech: adjective
Pronunciation: check dictionary audio; notice the stressed syllable
Meaning: extremely tired
Useful combinations: feel exhausted; completely exhausted; mentally exhausted
Example: I felt exhausted after studying all day.
Personal sentence: I often feel exhausted after finishing a difficult project.
Recall cue: How did you feel after yesterday's long study session?
```

You only need to practice one or two useful combinations initially. Add others when they appear again.

### Understand the vocabulary categories

| Category | Meaning | Example / learning action |
|---|---|---|
| Passive vocabulary | Language you recognize but cannot yet retrieve easily. | You understand “exhausted” in a story. More meaningful encounters may be enough. |
| Active vocabulary | Language you can choose and use for your own message. | You spontaneously say “I felt exhausted after class.” Practice retrieval and transfer. |
| Collocations | Words commonly used together. | `make a decision`, `take a break`, `heavy rain`. Save the combination. |
| Chunks | Useful multiword units you can retrieve together. | `As far as I know…`, `I'm looking forward to…`. Learn the surrounding pattern too. |
| Phrasal verbs | Verb + particle combinations with a particular meaning. | `get up`, `turn off`, `find out`. Learn one meaning in a sentence before adding others. |
| Word families | Related forms with different grammatical jobs. | `decide` → `decision`; `help` → `helpful`. Add a related form when useful, not every form automatically. |

Notice patterns: “I'm looking forward to **seeing** you,” “How about **going** out?” and “I'd rather **stay** home.” A chunk includes the grammar that makes it usable.

### Review through retrieval

Use a notebook, paper cards, or a spaced-review tool you already know. A notebook is sufficient.

1. Learn the item in today's input and make a personal sentence.
2. Try to recall it later in the same session without looking.
3. Review approximately **1, 3, 7, 14, and 30 days later**.
4. Use an image, situation, question, or sentence gap as the cue.
5. Say the expression, explain its meaning, and create a new sentence before checking.
6. If you fail, check the model, use it again, and review the next day.

Example card:

```text
Front: You need to rest after an hour of studying. What do you do?
Back: take a break
      I usually take a short break after an hour of studying.
```

Within a 12–15-minute vocabulary block, spend about **7–10 minutes retrieving old items** and the remainder adding today's items. Prioritize due reviews; postpone new items instead of extending the session.

Call an item increasingly active when you have retrieved it on separate days and used it in two different situations. Do not mistake recognizing a card for being able to use the phrase.

## 12. Thinking in English

Practice in the opening five minutes of each daily schedule, or briefly during speaking. Optional micro-habits can reinforce it.

| Level | When | Exercise |
|---|---|---|
| 1: objects and qualities | Weeks 1–2 | Look around: “a chair,” “my laptop,” “an open window,” “a quiet room.” Match words directly to objects. |
| 2: actions and routines | Weeks 3–4 | Narrate 5–8 actions: “I'm opening my laptop.” “I usually check my notes first.” |
| 3: needs, feelings, plans | Weeks 5–8 | Produce short thoughts: “I'm tired today.” “I need to finish this assignment.” “I think I'll study Java tonight.” |
| 4: connected internal monologue | Weeks 9–12 | Spend 1–2 minutes reasoning: “I'm not sure whether I should go out tonight because…” Then choose a plan. |

### A practical five-minute routine

1. Name three things you can see.
2. Describe two actions or observations.
3. State one feeling or need.
4. Give a reason or make a plan at your current level.
5. If a word is missing, describe the idea simply and continue.

If you notice yourself translating, return to an easier sentence that expresses the idea. Do not monitor every thought; that can interrupt fluency. It is normal to use Vietnamese for complex reasoning while basic English connections develop.

Once weekly, compare how long you can continue a familiar topic without mentally drafting the whole message in Vietnamese. Treat your impression as a reflection, not a precise measurement.

## 13. Error-Correction System

Mistakes give you information about what needs another meaningful attempt. During fluency practice, finish the message first. Stop immediately only when the listener cannot understand or when the activity specifically targets accuracy.

### Keep one small error notebook

| My sentence | Correct sentence | Error | Rule/pattern | New personal example | Review |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I am agree. | I agree. | Unnecessary `be`. | `agree` is a verb. | I agree with your suggestion. | Tomorrow / Sunday |
| Yesterday I go to class. | Yesterday I went to class. | Past event expressed with present form. | Finished event: past simple. | I went to the library yesterday. | Tomorrow / next week |
| She like music. | She likes music. | Missing third-person ending. | `he/she/it` + present-simple `-s`. | My sister likes cooking. | Tomorrow / Sunday |
| I look forward to meet you. | I look forward to meeting you. | Wrong verb form after this chunk. | `look forward to` + noun / `-ing`. | I'm looking forward to seeing my friends. | In three days |

Record **at most 1–3 useful issues from a session**, especially recurring ones. Your notebook should remain reviewable.

### Correction procedure

1. Select an error that affects meaning, occurs repeatedly, or concerns the current pattern.
2. Confirm the correction with a reliable source or capable reviewer.
3. Write a short explanation and a new personal example.
4. Say the corrected sentence naturally three times.
5. The next day, express the meaning again without looking.
6. On Sunday, practice the two or three most persistent issues in a new speaking/writing task.

Pronunciation entries can record “I omitted final /s/ in `likes`,” with a model phrase and recording. Avoid trying to fix your whole accent in one session.

Ask for feedback such as: “Was my meaning clear? Which two recurring errors should I work on? Show me a natural correction and let me try again.”

## 15. Weekly Two-Hour Schedule

**Each daily total is exactly 120 minutes, including one five-minute break.** This means 115 minutes of activity daily and 14 scheduled hours weekly. Across 90 days, it is 180 scheduled hours, including breaks.

In the ordinary weekly schedule, dedicated listening/reading total about **4½ hours**, and speaking/writing about **4½ hours**. Vocabulary, grammar, pronunciation, and reflection support both. Each day contains input and original output.

Use a timer and stop when a block ends. Carry an unfinished text forward; do not extend the session to finish every item. Breaks can move to a convenient point.

**Time rules:**

- Dictation occupies 5–8 minutes **within** listening. On intensive days, use it to investigate difficult lines rather than adding a separate full procedure.

- Replay a familiar short audio segment while commuting when practical.
- Search for a familiar topic in English.
- Read a short piece of software documentation when it helps with your actual work/study; skip difficult technical sections.
- Send a short ordinary message in English to a willing practice partner.
- Watch one enjoyable short English clip and say its main idea.
- Label a few useful objects mentally; do not turn every activity into a lesson.

Keep ordinary rest and enjoyment. Micro-habits should make English more present in your life, not create pressure to study constantly.

## 18. Weekly Review System

Use Sunday's scheduled blocks to review **evidence**, not just your feeling about the week.

### A simple self-assessment scale

| Rating | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 0 | I could not complete the task meaningfully yet. |
| 1 | I completed it with substantial support: transcript, full notes, or repeated help. |
| 2 | I completed it with limited support: a few keywords, one replay, or occasional clarification. |
| 3 | I completed it independently and used the skill/language in a changed situation. |

This is your own progress record, not a standardized proficiency score. Do not average it into a predicted exam result.

| Area | Sunday's check |
|---|---|
| Vocabulary | Recall 10–15 items sampled from this week and previous weeks; make new sentences for five. |
| Phrases | Use three chunks in a changed practical situation. |
| Listening | State the main idea and three details before viewing text; record how many replays/supports you needed. |
| Speaking | Complete the week's response length and answer two unprepared follow-ups. |
| Pronunciation | Check one target feature in five words/phrases and in spontaneous speech. |
| Reading | Explain the main idea and three details; note dictionary use. |
| Writing | Check whether your paragraph/message is understandable, connected, and improved after revision. |
| Grammar | Retrieve two target patterns and use them in personal sentences. |
| Errors | Identify two recurring issues and use their corrected forms without looking. |

Finish the planning block with:

```text
This week I can now…
The most useful phrase was…
I still need support with…
Next week I will focus on…
```

Choose **one communication goal and one accuracy goal**, such as “ask relevant follow-up questions” and “keep past-tense endings.” If comprehension repeatedly falls below a workable level, simplify next week's input. If vocabulary review overflows, reduce additions. If a task is consistently easy, remove notes or change the situation.

### Selecting material for a weekly topic

1. Open your main resource and choose the appropriate level/collection.
2. Select two recordings and two short texts closest to the topic; an exact topic match is unnecessary.

This small packet prevents daily browsing from replacing practice.

## 23. Expected Ability After Three Months

With consistent, meaningful practice, your realistic aim is **more reliable everyday English**, especially on familiar topics. Your starting strengths, feedback, material choices, and actual attendance affect the result. The following are working targets, not guaranteed proficiency certifications.

| Area | Practical end-of-phase target |
|---|---|
| Listening | Understand most of the main message and useful details in clear everyday conversations about familiar topics; identify a missed phrase, clarify it, and continue. |
| Speaking | Give a 2–3-minute explanation with reasons/examples and participate in a supported 8–10-minute everyday conversation without drafting every sentence. |
| Pronunciation | Produce more understandable final sounds, common endings, word/sentence stress, and phrasing; recognize common reductions without forcing them. |
| Reading | Follow easy stories and accessible general-interest articles of roughly 400–650 words with limited dictionary use; explain what you read. |
| Writing | Write a coherent 160–220-word everyday message, account, or opinion in one or two paragraphs and improve recurring problems through revision. |
| Vocabulary | Retrieve a growing personal set of useful words, collocations, and chunks across different situations; recognize many additional items passively. |
| Grammar | Use major everyday present, past, future, question, negative, comparison, and modal patterns with improving accuracy; use selected longer patterns when needed. |
| Sentence building | Connect ideas with reasons, sequence, contrast, examples, and simple conditions without relying on memorized whole answers. |
| Thinking | Name, describe, plan, and express familiar feelings/needs directly in basic English more often, while still using Vietnamese when helpful. |
| Independence | Select suitable input, diagnose a recurring difficulty, request clarification, review old language, and keep learning within a sustainable routine. |

Fast overlapping speech, unfamiliar accents, dense news, and abstract technical discussions may still require considerable support. Progress on everyday English does not imply effortless understanding of all native content.

At the end, keep a small portfolio: your first-day and latest recordings, an original/revised paragraph, a few listening/reading summaries, your most useful phrases, and your remaining recurring errors. These artifacts show what has become easier and what needs more practice.

> Become a better English user first. Let familiar English move gradually from “think → translate → construct → speak” toward “idea → English.”
